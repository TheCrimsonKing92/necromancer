# First-theater regional foundation

Status: **provisional regional standard for vertical-slice production**.

This document gives Fire Road, Siege Sovereign, and the first integrated
vertical slice enough shared culture and material history to look and sound as
though they occur in one part of Maldirin. It is not a declaration that every
proof character, proper noun, institution, god, or conflict is canonical.

The recommended working label is **the Veyward**. The label may be discarded.
The regional character described below is the part worth testing for durability.

## Fact status and provenance

Every content fact used from this brief should be understood under one of four
statuses:

- **A — Inherited constraint:** explicitly settled in `world.md`,
  `magic-and-environment.md`, `testament-continuity.md`, or the project-wide
  prose and presentation contracts. A source document being listed here does
  not promote every proposal within it to an inherited fact.
- **B — Proposed durable regional standard:** recommended as the shared default
  for this first continental region. Repetition may establish it as canon after
  playtesting.
- **C — Controlled deviation:** an authored locality, people, institution, or
  visitor may depart from the regional default for a stated reason. A deviation
  does not rewrite the region.
- **D — Disposable proof detail:** useful while producing the vertical slices,
  but safe to rename, replace, relocate, or remove.

Do not let a D fact silently become B through repeated placeholder copy. Record
the source and status of a new proper noun, visual motif, institution, language,
supernatural condition, or historical claim in the owning content definition.

## A. Inherited constraints

The following rules govern this region because they govern Maldirin:

- Maldirin is the world or broader setting and is probably multi-continental.
  The opening campaign occupies one contiguous continental theater; it is not
  the whole world.
- The theater contains layered, asymmetric powers: old sclerotic states,
  ambitious powers, secure small sovereignties, nonhuman civilizations, and
  interstitial country. Neither conquest nor recent collapse defines the age.
- The great decline is ancient. Present peoples possess long histories of their
  own and are not temporary squatters waiting for an ancient civilization to
  return.
- The present is an irregular period of movement, ambition, reclamation,
  exploitation, construction, and contact. These changes have many causes and
  are not uniformly beneficial.
- There is no common aspiration. Many people want to leave something worth
  inheriting, but differ over the inheritance, the heirs, and the acceptable
  cost. Others want survival, release, conquest, knowledge, pleasure, mastery,
  or an end that admits no successor.
- Ordinary reality is dependable. Magic is uncommon specialized labor;
  necromancy is a rare subset. Beginning necromancers remain exceptional people.
- Every magical act has a source, method, consequences, limits, and counters.
  Many practitioners carry meaningful agency with them. Place, equipment,
  preparation, permission, relationship, target, or opportunity matters only
  where the particular capability gives it reason to matter, and genuine limits
  bind NPCs as well as the player.
- Gods and cosmological Gods exist. Their agency is inherent; power, reach,
  portability, persistence, reliability, discretion, and individual limits vary
  independently. Some are situated because that is what they are, not because
  all divinity requires a local source.
- Human bodies, nonhuman bodies, manifestations, bound dead, and stranger forms
  require honest anatomy and visual rigs. A costume is not a substitute for a
  body plan.
- Necromantic specializations should remain mechanically and fictionally
  distinct. The current practice roster—including Grave Warden and the
  Ferryman/Ferrymaster concept—and the distinction between practice and
  tradition remain provisional design candidates rather than inherited world
  facts.
- Communities judge necromancy by act, body claim, consent, provenance,
  custom, harm, witness, and circumstance rather than one world morality meter.
- The world persists between testaments. Buildings, bodies, obligations,
  relationships, evidence, and damage remain until changed by authoritative
  advancement.
- Prose is concrete, dry, dark, and foreboding. Ordinary work and exact material
  consequences carry atmosphere before metaphysical explanation does.

## B. Proposed durable regional standards

### Regional premise: a continental hinge

The first region is a broad inhabited hinge between a broken limestone upland
and lower grain country. High roads, watercourses, quarry tracks, and military
approaches are forced through a limited number of passes and old works. Control
of a bridge, conduit, gate, store-yard, or dry road matters because geography
makes it matter, not because every threshold is secretly magical.

Cold rains and winter runoff arrive from the uplands. Late summer can still dry
the scrub and resin woods enough for fast fires. This supports Fire Road and the
failing conduit without making the entire region a sequence of elemental
disasters: weather, elevation, maintenance, and season distinguish the events.

The country is neither empty frontier nor continuous state. Towns, estates,
work settlements, old jurisdictions, god-secured enclaves, road communities,
and stretches of negotiated authority interlock. A traveler can cross three
different legal and social orders in several days without crossing a bright
line on a map.

### Historical and material depth

Most houses, tools, fields, carts, roads, and walls are products of present
peoples. Ancient works are exceptional in scale or method and visible as a
different construction stratum. The regional contrast is:

- human-scaled construction in materials a locality can obtain and maintain:
  timber, earth, brick, plaster, tile, slate, and worked stone in different
  proportions;
- repairs accumulated across generations;
- a few oversized conduits, causeways, culverts, foundations, and buried works;
- later structures fitted against, through, or around them; and
- failure that reveals old material rather than an automatic supernatural
  secret.

Some inherited works may belong to the Carrion Inheritance, captive powers, or
foreign construction, but most old masonry is simply old masonry. Characters
may disagree over whether one exceptional structure is a machine, body, prison,
ecology, or divine seat. The answer must be authored per structure.

The old age is not culturally remembered as yesterday's empire. Local masons
know joints, loads, mortar, drainage, and repair histories more reliably than
they know original makers. A functioning work belongs materially to the people
who have kept it functioning, even when another power asserts an older claim.

### Societies and political orders

Use four recurrent forms without treating them as an exhaustive constitution:

1. **Road and work towns.** Market towns and labor settlements govern immediate
   roads, water, stores, walls, and emergency work through officers and public
   assemblies of locally credible parties. Their authority is practical and can
   be divided, captured, or refused.
2. **Old charter powers.** Estates, cities, crowns, temples, and offices retain
   rights whose territorial reach exceeds their reliable presence. They can
   provide courts, trained officials, credit, troops, and large maintenance,
   while remaining slow, extractive, or internally divided.
3. **Small secured sovereignties.** A locality may remain unusually independent
   because of terrain, a resident god, a nonhuman constitution, an inherited
   work, or a narrow indispensable service. Security does not imply wealth,
   benevolence, or expansion.
4. **Mobile and interstitial powers.** Armies, caravans, seasonal labor,
   migrants, dispossessed households, religious processions, and incorporated
   peoples can exercise real authority without owning a continuous territory.

No one institution resolves every dispute. Household claim, work authority,
local office, charter, shrine custody, military necessity, and safe-conduct may
overlap. A scene should identify which participants actually possess each kind
of authority instead of invoking generic law.

Local customs exist before the player arrives. If a town may requisition timber
during a flood, a credible local actor can propose or contest that custom; an
outsider does not remind an entire community of its own basic law. The player
may know a custom through personal or inherited local history, or learn it from
people, records, observation, and prior scenes. Invoking it still depends on
standing, interpretation, witnesses, and opposition. A custom can create
compensation and later claims rather than free resources.

### Households, inhabitants, and social life

Humans are the numerical majority in the initial vertical-slice localities.
Obviously nonhuman people are rare in the theater, and open integration into a
predominantly human community is rarer still. Nonhuman people whose nature is
not immediately visible may live openly as individuals, conceal it, or be known
only within particular relationships until circumstances change. None of these
patterns is mandatory for every people or locality.

`Household` has no single regional size or constitution. One society may treat
it as a legal and productive unit containing extended kin, dependants,
apprentices, servants, lodgers, fostered children, or long-term companions;
another may organize domestic, economic, and legal belonging differently. Do
not infer one couple, one building, one authority, or one political opinion from
the term. Define what it means in the owning locality before making it a rule or
counting unit.

People belong to several relationships at once: household, occupation, ward,
shrine, patron, military obligation, road community, language, and personal
attachment. A person's job provides competence and material interests; it does
not consume their personality.

Before establishing a resident nonhuman people, give it a cultural and
anatomical dossier rather than generating a random body and treating appearance
as culture. If an individual occupies an ordinary local role, make that
belonging historically and socially credible; the region is not required to
include such a case merely to demonstrate one.

### Languages and names

The durable language pattern is multilingual rather than one invented accent:

- a regional trade and administrative common permits travel, courts, markets,
  and mixed work crews;
- local valley, town, estate, and household speech remains audible in names,
  idiom, and private conversation;
- old works may carry one or more inscriptional languages that specialists read
  incompletely; and
- foreign armies, migrants, nonhuman peoples, and religious communities may use
  their own languages, with interpreters and partial fluency where credible.

Do not write accents phonetically. Distinguish voices by what speakers notice,
sentence rhythm, terms of address, willingness to name uncertainty, and
occupational vocabulary.

For regional human names, use clear two-part forms as the baseline: a given name
of one to three syllables and a short family, household, or locative name.
`Sera Vale`, `Toman Rusk`, `Maelin Orr`, `Eda Pell`, `Oren Vask`, and `Iven
Saye` occupy a compatible working register. This is a sound palette, not a
closed generator. Avoid mandatory apostrophes, ornamental doubled letters, and
an epithet on every person. Attach office when it matters: `Toman Rusk, master
mason` is more useful than a fantasy sobriquet.

Foreign naming should follow a separately defined pattern rather than merely
adding harsher consonants. `Halwen Sar` can remain a D example until the
speaker's language and polity are specified.

Necromancer use-names should fit a chosen cultural history. They need not sound
gothic or reveal practice. A true name is protected because of specific risks
and relationships, not because ordinary regional names are incantations.

### Dress and material culture

The region has no universal costume or palette. Dress begins with a person's
local climate, available fibers and hides, habitual work, travel, status, bodily
needs, and the care they can afford:

- the colder, wetter uplands favor insulating layers, weather protection, sure
  footing, and garments that survive stone, animals, and abrasive work;
- the lower grain country answers hotter and drier seasons, field labor, dust,
  insects, and periodic smoke with its own cuts, weights, coverings, and work
  clothes;
- hinge roads and market towns mix local dress with equipment adapted for
  travel, rain, carts, storage, and several climates; and
- occupation should remain visible through credible carried objects and wear:
  key rings, chalk reels, hooks, ledgers, mallets, splint cases, aprons, rope,
  measuring staffs, and tool satchels where the work calls for them.

Color follows material access and meaning. Local dyes, undyed fibers, imported
cloth, sumptuary rules, mourning, office, cult, military livery, wealth, care,
and reuse determine what colors appear and who can wear them. Saturation is not
automatically supernatural, and the UI's grave-violet does not become the color
of all regional magic. A production palette belongs to a named locality or
group, with an economic and cultural reason, rather than to the whole theater.

Common weapons are also tools or credible military equipment: knives, axes,
hooks, bows, spears, clubs, hammers, short swords, shields, and pole tools.
Exceptional weapons should reveal maker, use, and material rather than grow
spikes merely to look rare.

### Settlements and architecture

There is no single settlement kit for both geographic strata. Architecture
follows available earth, timber, reeds, brick clay, lime, slate, tile, and
quarried stone; local weather; inhabitants' bodies and work; defense; inherited
craft; and the structures that earlier peoples left behind. Upland quarry towns,
lower-country farm settlements, road markets, military works, and a migrated or
conquered community should remain visibly different even when they trade
materials and techniques.

Across those differences, working settlements still need credible yards,
stores, water, drainage, heat, animals, roads, waste handling, and access. Public
structures should be distinguished by their real function, scale, durability,
access, ownership, and maintenance rather than universal gothic ornament.
Repairs should use materials and skills the locality can actually obtain; older
forms may persist because people value them, because replacement is expensive,
or because later construction has grown around them.

Settlements should look inhabited: stacked fuel, washing, refuse control, pens,
water vessels, worn desire paths, work in progress, and protected stores. A
fallen world is not an empty world. Poverty and decay vary by household and
locality rather than coating every surface equally.

When play expects an ordinary or understood work to be navigated, its actionable
physical relationships must be legible on sight. A conduit scene shows water,
piers, access, load-bearing structures, and dry routes; a fire scene shows fuel,
breaks, water, wagon routes, bridges, and refuge. Bizarre, alien, damaged, or
genuinely unknown works may resist visual interpretation, but that uncertainty
must be an authored condition the player can investigate—not accidental
confusion caused by an attractive but unusable painting. Actors still need
credible ground, scale, and access.

### Livelihoods and economy

The regional economy is supported by several ordinary and inherited advantages:

- grain and hardy field crops in the lower country;
- sheep, goats, draft animals, dairying, and household gardens;
- quarrying, masonry, lime, ceramics, and repair trades near the upland;
- managed timber, charcoal, resin, pitch, and cart work;
- mills, water distribution, warehouses, road services, cloth work, and local
  metal repair; and
- long-distance movement through the hinge routes.

Inherited works can produce local surplus, but their catchments are bounded.
One maintained conduit may support a town and several fields while a nearby
village remains poor or dry. Damage, monopoly, blocked access, or absent skilled
labor can degrade the benefit without changing the whole region's modifier.

Most people are not living in uninterrupted misery. Some households possess
tools, stored grain, livestock, decorated clothing, credit, and expectations of
a future. Others are precarious, indebted, displaced, or trapped below failing
infrastructure. Hardship matters because there are lives and projects worth
losing.

Coin, barter, obligation, ration, requisition, service, and credit coexist.
Black obols remain occult or specialist currency unless separately established;
they are not the default money in a grain yard. Use ordinary coin terminology
until denominations are decided. A transaction should reveal who owns the good,
who needs it, what scarcity changes the terms, and what obligation survives the
exchange.

### Religion, gods, and Gods

There is no uniform regional pantheon. The durable pattern is a layered religious
landscape:

- household and place gods may be present through hearth, spring, workshop,
  bridge, field, species, office, language, or repeated custom;
- town and occupational cults maintain public relationships and may disagree
  with household practice;
- temples, itinerant orders, and old liturgies address cosmological Gods whose
  total existence is not reducible to a local shrine; and
- skeptics, rival explanations, mixed observance, neglected offices, and
  practical participation all exist without making gods imaginary.

A god receives a body, voice, sign, office, custodian, or material mode of
participation appropriate to that god when it enters a scene. That representation
makes divine character and action legible; it does not necessarily make action
possible. Avoid an omnipresent disembodied narrator unless that is specifically
the god's mode. Dialogue with a god should expose character, scale, limitation,
and desire—not only terms in an abstract contract.

Some domestic and local gods are inseparable from a hearth, bridge, field,
species, or town. They may be nearly sovereign there and absent elsewhere.
Others can travel or act at distance. A carried coal, kept stone, toll, office,
or custodian may extend a particular god's participation, improve communication,
or expose it to mortal action; do not assume the object powers the god. A God
may act across the region without local worship. Its body, aspect, adherent, or
site should usually make its attention precise, persistent, intelligible, or
vulnerable rather than grant basic permission to act.

Religion remains part of ordinary life without making every guild, court, or
farm a disguised cult.

### Magic and necromancy

The region can plausibly support a few non-necromantic specializations without
making practitioners common. These examples deliberately use different sources
and degrees of portability:

- a masonry specialist whose altered hearing and trained percussion travel with
  them, while tuned iron or familiar stone improves precision;
- a weather or water worker who can carry a modest instrument, exploit a
  current or season, or belong to a lineage genuinely bound to one catchment;
- a healer whose rare work extends living processes through bodily aptitude and
  trained perception but still requires diagnosis, clean tools, time, and
  ordinary care;
- a shrine practitioner whose action depends on the character and reach of a
  particular god, perhaps extended through an office or carried participation;
  and
- a protective craftsperson whose learned work becomes portable in finite
  charges, materials, and coverage.

These are examples, not exhaustive schools. A town usually has no full roster
of magical specialists. A practitioner encountered at ordinary work should
possess whatever capacities, tools, preparations, patrons, collaborators, and
favored circumstances make their competence credible. Do not add a local
provider to an otherwise portable art merely to conform it to regional flavor.

Regional attitudes toward necromancy use a cautious but plural baseline. The
current prototype practices offer useful examples of social consequences, not a
promise that each will remain in the starting roster:

- quieting dangerous dead and preserving a grave or refuge are often tolerated
  when claim and harm are clear;
- Medium testimony is sought and disputed, especially when consent or identity
  is uncertain;
- a Bonebinder's servant forces questions of body claim, labor, recognition,
  and entry into domestic or civic space;
- Renderer work can resemble accepted medical or material trades while crossing
  the clearest taboos around recent human remains; and
- funerary passage signs may promise safe conduct in one locality and threaten
  final removal in another, if that locality recognizes the passage at all.

Very few ordinary people have met a necromancer. Characters should respond from
local history and the visible act rather than instantly identifying every
practice technique. Clothes alone should not function as a universal
`necromancer` uniform.

### Travel and warfare

Travel follows named roads, work tracks, passes, bridges, ferries, and seasonal
routes. News and armies move at material speeds. A route's safety depends on
maintenance, weather, authority, traffic, and current actors rather than one
global danger level.

Roadside habitation is normal near defensible water, bridges, shrines, mills,
quarries, and markets. Long empty stretches exist where terrain, war, exhausted
workings, or failed infrastructure explain them.

Armies require carts, fodder, water, camps, scouts, crossings, repair labor,
interpreters, surgeons, and permission or coercion. Magic can change logistics
but does not abolish them. A foreign column should be visible through supply,
camp organization, civilian relations, and doctrine before every soldier is
treated as a combat encounter.

Regional warfare includes levies, household defense, professional retainers,
town watches, mercenary companies, raiding, siege work, and exceptional armies
attached to supernatural powers. Conquest is a live possibility, not the only
source of conflict. Soldiers remain people with home ties, injuries, pay,
language, and competing obligations.

### Visual direction and silhouette grammar

The art must be navigation-grade before it is atmospheric. For each isometric
or dimetric scene:

1. Define the authoritative node graph, entrances, elevation changes, hazards,
   occlusion, actor scale, and likely state changes before painting.
2. Use one projection and scale family across connected scenes. Establish a
   human reference figure and door, cart, stair, bridge, and animal scale sheet.
3. Keep changeable actors, bodies, wagons, fire, barricades, water, doors, loot,
   and damage out of the immutable ground plate.
4. Export ground, architecture, foreground occlusion, authoritative state
   layers, and lighting/effects separately where they can change or cover an
   actor.
5. Make destinations legible as physical places—road forks, doors, clear work
   bays, stair landings, gate mouths—not as permanent movement glyphs.
6. Give every standing position visible ground and foot room. Test all legal
   multi-actor formations before approving the plate.
7. Compose a dedicated narrow-screen view or authored crop. Do not stack a wide
   map's nodes into abstract mobile boxes and call it the same scene.

At a 1600×900 reference plate, primary-plane human figures should generally read
at roughly 110–135 pixels tall before UI labels. Depth scaling may vary only
within an authored band. A giant creature is giant relative to doors, carts,
trees, and people, not merely because its sprite file is larger.

Silhouette should communicate role before labels:

- local workers carry low, layered, tool-broken silhouettes;
- officials are distinguished by maintained cloth, seals, keys, staffs, or
  escort, not clean hands and crowns alone;
- soldiers show formation, carried load, armor doctrine, and livery;
- practitioners show the actual implements and prepared materials of their work;
- persistent servants show anatomy, repairs, equipment, and accumulated damage;
  and
- nonhumans and manifestations receive body-specific silhouettes and motion,
  never a human sprite with an effect placed over it.

### Animation and dialogue presentation

Movement and action should show mass and intention without becoming a continuous
simulation:

- locomotion begins with orientation and weight shift, plants feet at the
  destination, and resolves one actor or initiative group at a time;
- attacks and work actions use anticipation, commitment, contact or transfer,
  target response, and recovery in authoritative order;
- tools remain in hands, loads stay attached to carriers, and cloth or equipment
  receives one bounded secondary settle;
- injured people favor the affected region; servants and odd anatomies use their
  own gait and failure poses; and
- idle life uses occasional authored settles on focus or phase entry rather
  than a perpetual animation loop.

Dialogue should preserve the in-world scene behind the speakers. Reframe and
subdue the current location, then bring one or two character figures or derived
portraits forward with their actual clothing, equipment, wounds, and current
posture. A minimal reusable set is guarded/resting, intent, strained or wounded,
and one scene-specific reaction. Return to the exact prior scene focus after the
exchange.

Dialogue is an encounter between people, not a delivery box for lore. Sera can
know who has refused an order and what the fire will reach; Toman can name a
load, tool, and compromise; Halwen can reveal doctrine through the way she asks
for passage. None should explain the whole setting because the player clicked
their portrait.

## C. Controlled and common deviations

Use deviations to make the region plural, not random:

- **Climate:** upland winter rain, sheltered grain lowlands, dry resin scrub,
  fog basins, and snowbound passes can coexist because elevation and exposure
  differ. State season and locality.
- **Wealth:** a conduit-fed town may be prosperous while an adjacent quarry
  settlement declines after the road shifts. Identify the surplus engine and
  its catchment.
- **Architecture:** timber-rich villages, slate towns, earth-walled farmsteads,
  military stonework, and nonhuman forms may depart from the baseline according
  to material supply, history, body plan, and defense.
- **Authority:** town assembly, estate officer, shrine custodian, army marshal,
  household elder, work guild, and resident god may each dominate one locality.
  Do not procedurally assign all of them to every place.
- **Necromancy posture:** one burial settlement may license physical mortuary
  seals and ban bound servants; another may accept an ancestor servant and
  reject testimony obtained through hosting a dead guest. Give the divergence a
  material history.
- **Magic:** a catchment may strengthen or be essential to one water-worker; a
  kiln town may train heat-workers who later carry prepared heat; a godhold may
  sustain a cult practice whose reach depends on that particular god. State
  whether the locality supplies training, materials, patronage, affinity, a
  substitute, or a true requirement. Loss of a site or relationship limits only
  capabilities that actually depend upon it.
- **Language:** market towns are multilingual; isolated valleys or secure
  sovereignties may preserve another language. Translation has a speaker,
  document, cost, or limitation.
- **Nonhuman settlement:** a nonhuman polity may be locally sovereign or an
  ordinary town participant. Its buildings, tools, clothes, and animation follow
  its anatomy and history rather than the human kit.
- **Old work:** one conduit may be a dead supernatural body; another is human
  engineering; a third is a foreign machine. Reuse visual age strata, not one
  hidden explanation.
- **Present movement:** refugees, armies, pilgrims, merchants, returning powers,
  precursor pockets, and opportunists may enter the region independently. Each
  requires provenance and should not secretly serve the central mover by
  default.

A content pool should not choose deviations as interchangeable flavor tags.
Select a validated locality package whose economy, architecture, people,
institutions, magical population and opportunities, and visual assets agree.

## D. Disposable vertical-slice details

The following details may be used for continuity during presentation work, but
they do not become durable regional facts without a separate decision:

- **The Veyward** as the region's name, `Veyic` or any other language label, and
  the soot/limestone/oat/lichen/madder palette used in the first Fire Road art.
- North Vey, the Black Mile, Saint Orra, county reeves, county burial law, and
  black obols from the original proving ground.
- Sera Vale, Toman Rusk, Maelin Orr, Halwen Sar, Ditch-Hand, and every current
  title, biography, pronoun, or institutional allegiance not established in an
  owning character record.
- The exact Fire Road household and grain counts, resin-scrub ignition, bridge,
  ash hound, foreign host, air-speaker, subject ordinance, and seasonal bargain.
- The exact failing conduit, concealed coalition, foreign column, water reeve,
  Siege Sovereign body, road price, and attack beneath the arch.
- Any implication that Fire Road and Siege Sovereign occur in the same season,
  town, route, war, or immediate campaign sequence.
- Any implication that `Sovereign`, `coalition`, `subject ordinance`, `answer`,
  `ledger`, `tithe`, or another proof term names a universal institution or
  occult mechanism.

Proofs may share materials, garments, building techniques, names, or colors only
when geography, trade, migration, government, religion, or another authored
relationship explains the resemblance. Production consistency comes from the
regional causal rules and visual scale contract, not from dressing two distinct
localities from one kit. Plot-specific facts stay in their own packs.

## Practical authoring checklist

### Region and locality

- [ ] Name the continent/theater relation without implying this is all Maldirin.
- [ ] Identify the locality's terrain, season, water, fuel, food, and road access.
- [ ] Define its relevant societies and what `household`, kinship, residence,
  citizenship, and obligation mean where those concepts enter play.
- [ ] Name its ordinary surplus engine or explain why it has none.
- [ ] Bound inherited infrastructure by catchment, access, capacity, and repair.
- [ ] Derive clothing, colors, tools, and construction from climate, work,
  resources, access, history, body plan, and specific social meanings.
- [ ] Identify actual authorities and the source and limit of each authority.
- [ ] State one regional default and any controlled deviation being used.
- [ ] Give every unusual people, god, army, institution, and old work provenance.
- [ ] Separate fact, character knowledge, actor belief, evidence, and rumor.

### Scene and navigation art

- [ ] Author movement nodes, edges, elevation, entrances, hazards, and occlusion
  before the final plate.
- [ ] Verify scale against a reference human, door, cart, animal, and tree.
- [ ] Ensure every legal actor formation has ground and readable separation.
- [ ] Represent every named objective or remove the name from action copy.
- [ ] Keep changeable bodies, items, actors, hazards, and damage on state layers.
- [ ] Provide before/after states for every persistent visible consequence.
- [ ] Give desktop and narrow-screen compositions equal semantic coverage.
- [ ] Keep focus, target, cost, prospect, health, and intent available without
  leaving permanent labels across the physical scene.
- [ ] Test dialogue crops against the same authoritative scene state.

### Character and creature

- [ ] Record culture, language, household/affiliation, occupation, authority,
  current need, knowledge, belief, and relationship separately.
- [ ] Give the character interests beyond their job and immediate quest use.
- [ ] Follow the owning culture's name register; mark a foreign or use-name as
  such rather than treating it as decoration.
- [ ] Build clothing from climate, work, status, care, and material access.
- [ ] Show carried equipment and practice tools on compatible mounts.
- [ ] Define anatomy, scale, gait, reach, wound regions, and defeat pose before
  generating an odd entity's art.
- [ ] Provide guarded, intent, strained/wounded, and scene-specific dialogue
  states for recurring people.
- [ ] Make enemies, allies, civilians, and servants equally subject to their
  bodies, equipment, environment, and injuries.

### Item and equipment

- [ ] Name maker or production context, material, ordinary purpose, repair
  method, provenance, present claim, and current condition where relevant.
- [ ] Define field silhouette and wear/mount rules as well as inventory art.
- [ ] Distinguish common quality through care and construction, not only rarity
  color.
- [ ] Keep occult property tied to a specific relationship or history.
- [ ] Do not use black obols or grave materials as the price language for an
  ordinary exchange without a regional reason.

### Prose and dialogue

- [ ] Establish concrete place, present work, human interest, and immediate
  disturbance before asking for a decision.
- [ ] Let a speaker know only what their experience, office, and evidence allow.
- [ ] Use occupational terms only when the context makes them intelligible.
- [ ] Prefer recognizable verbs and nouns to invented titles doing exposition's
  work.
- [ ] Trace every pronoun and read interpolated variants in rendered context.
- [ ] Keep one strange image only when it clarifies a material or emotional fact.
- [ ] Give gods and major actors voice, limitation, and desire rather than only
  mechanical terms.
- [ ] Remove solved actions and revise remaining dialogue after the scene changes.
- [ ] Ensure result prose depicts an experienced action and visible consequence,
  not a silent state mutation or a report of something the player did not see.

## First embodiment recommendation

Use the two proof families to test different parts of the same foundation:

- **Fire Road:** regional vernacular settlement, ordinary households, grain and
  cart economy, dry-season hazard, bridge-scale navigation, a local authority,
  servant labor, civilian initiative, and visible aftermath.
- **Siege Sovereign:** inherited public work, repair culture, vertical
  navigation, local and foreign authority, army logistics, nonhuman scale,
  dialogue staging, combat choreography, and contested long-term consequence.

Before treating either as a campaign chapter, add one quieter recurring place
using the same regional kit: a market yard, masonry quarter, household court, or
road inn where people can trade, recover, argue, remember prior acts, and become
recognizable without an immediate catastrophe. That third location will reveal
whether the foundation describes a living region or only two attractive crises.
